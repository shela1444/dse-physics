📌 1. Key Concepts: Force, Mass & Weight

Mass (kg): measure of inertia, scalar, constant everywhere.
Weight (N): gravitational force on an object, W = mg, vector, varies with g.
On Earth, g ≈ 9.81 m/s²; on Moon, g ≈ 1/6 of Earth's.
✅ Past paper classic: "An object weighs 120 N on Earth. On Moon (g = 1/6 gE), mass = 120/9.81 ≈ 12.2 kg, weight = 20 N." (e.g. 2009 CE Q8)

📐 2. Free‑Body Diagrams (FBD) – Essential Rules

What is a free‑body diagram? It shows all the forces acting directly on a chosen object, represented as vectors from a point.

Two critical points:

  1. Only forces acting directly on the object should be included. Forces that the object exerts on other objects are not drawn.
  2. Indicate the direction of velocity (if known) beside the FBD. This helps you choose coordinate axes and decide the sign of forces when applying Newton’s 2nd law. The direction of acceleration is not always the same as velocity, but defining axes consistently avoids sign errors.

Common forces: weight (vertically down), normal reaction (⊥ to surface), tension (along string), friction (opposes motion/tendency).

📌 Example: A box being pushed to the right on a rough floor. FBD includes weight, normal, applied force (right), friction (left). Velocity is to the right → choose +x to the right. Then ΣFx = Fapp – f = ma.

🧠 3. Newton's Laws of Motion

3.1 Newton's First Law – Law of Inertia

An object remains at rest, or continues to move with constant velocity, unless acted upon by a net external force.

Implications:

  • If Fnet = 0, a = 0 (constant velocity, including rest).
  • Inertia: resistance to change in motion; mass measures inertia.
  • Real‑life examples: coin on cardboard, passenger lurching forward when car brakes.

3.2 Newton's Second Law – F = ma

Fnet = m a

Net force (vector sum of all forces) = mass × acceleration. Direction of a is same as Fnet.

Key points:

  • Unit: 1 N = 1 kg·m/s².
  • For problems: choose axes, resolve forces, write ΣFx = max, ΣFy = may.
  • Constant net force → constant acceleration.

3.3 Newton's Third Law – Action & Reaction

If object A exerts a force on object B, then B exerts an equal and opposite force on A.

Forces act on different objects, same magnitude, opposite direction.

⚠️ Common misconception: Action‑reaction pairs do not cancel because they act on different bodies. E.g., weight (Earth on block) and normal (table on block) are not an action‑reaction pair.

Example (Class Work 9 / 2003 CE Q12)

Child sitting on chair: action‑reaction pair is child pushes chair down and chair pushes child up (normal reaction). Weight of child and normal reaction act on the same body → they can balance each other when a = 0, but they are not action‑reaction.

🤔 4. Which Newton's Law to Use? – A Practical Guide

Students often struggle to choose the correct law when explaining a scenario. Use this decision guide:

Situation / Question FocusPrimary Law(s)Explanation / Key Phrase
Object at rest or moving with constant velocity (including "uniform motion", "balanced forces")First Law"No net force → state of motion unchanged." Or "Forces are balanced."
Object accelerating (speeding up, slowing down, changing direction) – need to relate force to accelerationSecond Law"Net force = mass × acceleration. The direction of acceleration is the same as the net force."
Two objects interacting – explaining why forces come in pairs, e.g., why a rocket moves forward, why a gun recoils, why a swimmer pushes water backwardThird Law"Action and reaction forces act on different objects, equal in magnitude, opposite in direction."
Why a passenger lurches forward when car brakesFirst Law"The passenger continues moving forward due to inertia (no net force on passenger until seatbelt/seat exerts force)."
Why a box slides to a stop on a rough surfaceSecond Law (or First Law + friction)"Friction provides a net force opposite to motion, causing deceleration (negative acceleration)."
Why a helicopter can hover (or a balloon stays up)Third Law + First Law"Downward thrust on air → upward reaction force on helicopter. When this equals weight, net force = 0 → hover."
Why objects of different masses fall with same acceleration (in vacuum)Second Law (and independence of mass)"Weight = mg, net force = mg, so a = g, independent of mass."
Explaining why a scale reading changes in a liftSecond Law"Apparent weight = normal reaction. ΣF = ma gives R – mg = ma → R = m(g+a)."

General rule: Use the First Law to explain why motion doesn't change; use the Second Law to calculate or explain changes in motion; use the Third Law when describing interactions between two objects.

🧩 5. Applications – Solving Problems

5.1 Uniform Motion (a = 0) → Fnet = 0

Example (Class Work 13 / 1998 CE Q6): Car towed at constant speed 8 m/s, friction 500 N. Since a = 0, net force = 0 → tension = friction = 500 N.

5.2 Non‑uniform Motion (a ≠ 0)

Horizontal rough surface (Class Work 15): Block 1 kg, F = 5 N, a = 2 m/s². Fnet = ma = 2 N → friction = F – Fnet = 5 – 2 = 3 N.

Vertical motion / lift problems (apparent weight):

SituationApparent weight (R)Relation
At rest or constant velocitymgR = mg
Accelerating upward> mgR = m(g + a)
Accelerating downward< mgR = m(g – a)
Free fall (a = g)0weightlessness
✅ Past paper (2002/II/7): man 80 kg, scale reads 600 N → R = 600 N, mg = 784 N → net force = 600 – 784 = –184 N → a = –184/80 = –2.3 m/s² (downward). So statement (3) correct.

5.3 Connected Bodies (Atwood machine, horizontal string)

Horizontal smooth table (Class Work 23 / 1992 CE Q9): Two blocks (2 kg, 3 kg) connected, force 2 N on 2 kg block. Find acceleration and tension.
System: Fnet = 2 N, total mass = 5 kg → a = 0.4 m/s². For 3 kg block: T = m a = 3×0.4 = 1.2 N. (Past paper 1992/II/9 answer: 1.2 N)

Two blocks with forces in opposite directions (2008/II/5): equal mass, 10 N left on A, 4 N right on B, smooth. Find tension.
Let common acceleration a. For A: T – 10 = m a; for B: 4 – T = m a. Subtract: (T–10) – (4–T) = 0 → 2T –14 = 0 → T = 7 N.

5.4 Pulley Systems (vertical)

Two masses m₁ and m₂ (m₁ > m₂) over frictionless pulley. Acceleration a = (m₁ – m₂)g / (m₁ + m₂), tension T = (2 m₁ m₂ g)/(m₁ + m₂).

a = (m₁ - m₂)g / (m₁ + m₂)

⛰️ 6. Inclined Planes

For a block on an incline at angle θ (smooth):

  • Component of weight parallel to plane: mg sinθ
  • Normal reaction: N = mg cosθ
  • If rough and moving, friction f = μN (μ = coefficient of friction).
Key equations:
- Net force down plane (no friction): Fnet = mg sinθ → a = g sinθ.
- Net force up plane with applied force F (parallel): F – mg sinθ – f = ma.
- Equilibrium (rest or constant velocity): net force = 0.

Example (Class Work 38 / 2002 CE Q6)

Block 0.5 kg slides down rough 30° incline with a = 3 m/s². Find friction.
mg sinθ = 0.5×9.81×sin30° ≈ 2.45 N. Fnet = m a = 1.5 N.
Fnet = mg sinθ – f → 1.5 = 2.45 – f → f = 0.95 N ≈ 1 N. (Matches 2002/II/6)

Example (Class Work 41 / 2021 DSE style)

Block slides down 30° incline at uniform speed → net force = 0 → friction = mg sin30° = 0.5 mg. Statement (2) correct.

🔁 7. Vector Addition & Resolution

7.1 Resultant of forces

Graphical (tip-to-tail) or algebraic (component method). For equilibrium, sum of forces = 0.

Resultant R = √(ΣFx² + ΣFy²)

Example (2000/II/6):

Three forces: F₁, F₂, 10 N at angles. Object in equilibrium. Use ΣFx = 0, ΣFy = 0. Solve for F₁, F₂.

7.2 Tension in symmetrical strings

Midpoint of rope with weight W, angle 2θ between two halves (i.e., each half makes angle θ with horizontal). Vertical equilibrium: 2T sinθ = W → T = W/(2 sinθ).

📌 Past paper (1999/II/5): T‑shirt weight 2 N, ∠ACB = 150°, so each half makes 15° with horizontal? Actually angle with vertical is 75°. Use 2T cos75° = W → T = 2/(2 cos75°) ≈ 3.86 N. (Answer C)

🧪 9. Experimental Skills

  • Newton’s second law experiment: friction‑compensated runway (slope to cancel friction). Trolley pulled by weights, measure a using ticker‑tape / light gates. Plot a vs F, slope = 1/m.
  • Effect of mass: Keep net force constant, vary total mass, a ∝ 1/m.
✅ In past papers, they ask to identify which trials keep mass constant (to find a ∝ F) or force constant (to find a ∝ 1/m).

⚠️ 10. Common Mistakes & Exam Strategies

  • FBD mistakes: including forces that act on other objects, or forgetting to show velocity direction to guide sign convention.
  • Action‑reaction confusion: weight and normal reaction are NOT action‑reaction (they act on same object).
  • Sign convention: define positive direction (e.g., upward positive for lift, direction of motion for horizontal) before writing ΣF = ma.
  • Friction direction: always opposes relative motion (or tendency). On an incline, when block slides down, friction up the plane.
  • Units: ensure mass in kg, acceleration in m/s² → force in N.
Quick Problem‑Solving Flow:
1. Draw FBD – include only forces on the object, and indicate velocity direction.
2. Choose coordinate axes (usually x along acceleration).
3. Write ΣFx = max, ΣFy = may.
4. Solve equations algebraically.

📖 11. Formula Summary

Newton's second law: Fnet = m a
Weight: W = m g
Inclined plane (smooth): a = g sinθ, N = mg cosθ
Pulley (Atwood): a = (m₁ – m₂)g/(m₁ + m₂), T = (2m₁m₂g)/(m₁+m₂)
Apparent weight (lift): R = m(g ± a) (upward +, downward –)
Resolution: Fx = F cosθ, Fy = F sinθ
